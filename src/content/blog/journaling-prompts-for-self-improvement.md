A blank page is a strange thing to stare at after a long day. Good journaling prompts for self-improvement do one job: they turn "write something meaningful" into a question small enough to answer before you lose interest. The 30 below are ours. We wrote them to be answered in two or three lines, on a phone, by someone who is tired.

They come in five groups of six. You do not need to go in order. Pick the group that matches the week you are having, and skip any prompt that feels like a test.

## How to use these prompts

- **One prompt a day is plenty.** Two lines count.
- **Answer honestly, not impressively.** Nobody else reads this.
- **Re-use prompts.** Answering the same question a month later is where patterns show up.
- **Stop if a prompt stirs up something heavy** and you would rather not sit with it alone tonight.

If you are brand new to journaling, start with our [7-day plan for starting a digital journal](/blog/how-to-start-a-digital-journal/) first. These prompts are for week two onwards.

## The 30 prompts

### Noticing (where you are now)

1. What took most of my energy today, and did it deserve it?
2. When did I feel most like myself this week?
3. What am I tolerating that I have stopped noticing?
4. Which part of today would I happily repeat tomorrow?
5. What is a worry I keep returning to? Write it in one sentence.
6. What did my body tell me today that I ignored?

### Habits (what you keep doing)

7. What did I do on autopilot today, good or bad?
8. Which small habit would make next week easier?
9. What usually happens right before I skip something I meant to do?
10. If I could only keep three routines, which three?
11. What is one thing I do "just in case" that I could drop?
12. Where did my phone time go today, roughly?

### Plans (if-then thinking)

13. If I feel like quitting this week, then what will I do instead?
14. What is the next physical step on the thing I am avoiding?
15. What would "good enough" look like for my biggest task tomorrow?
16. Which commitment should I say no to, and how will I say it?
17. When exactly will I do the thing I keep postponing?
18. What will I do the next time the same problem shows up?

### People (who is around you)

19. Who made today easier, and do they know?
20. Whose message have I been avoiding, and why?
21. What do I want people to say about how I treated them this year?
22. Who do I miss, and what is one small way to reach them?
23. Where did I hold back an opinion today? Was that right?
24. What would I like to ask for help with?

### Gratitude (what is already working)

25. What are three ordinary things that went right today?
26. What is something I own that quietly makes life easier?
27. Who taught me something useful, even by accident?
28. What part of my routine am I glad I kept?
29. What is a problem I had a year ago that I no longer have?
30. What small thing felt good today?

The "Plans" group uses a format researchers call implementation intentions: "if this situation happens, then I will do that". A 2021 meta-analysis in [Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892/) found that combining this kind of if-then planning with picturing obstacles had a small-to-medium effect on reaching goals. Small is the right word. It helps, it does not do the work for you.

## What research says about writing like this

A 2025 systematic review in [PLoS One](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12094736/) looked at positive writing, gratitude lists included, in people without a clinical diagnosis. It found the clearest gains in wellbeing and positive mood, with weaker and less consistent effects elsewhere, and called many of the studies low quality. The US Department of Veterans Affairs' [therapeutic journaling guide](https://www.va.gov/WHOLEHEALTHLIBRARY/docs/Therapeutic-Journaling.pdf) describes a more intensive method, writing about one emotional experience for 15 to 20 minutes across several days.

In short: prompts are a reasonable tool for reflection. They are not therapy, and nobody should promise that a list of questions will change your life.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many prompts should I answer per day?

One. If you have more to say, keep writing, but do not make three prompts the minimum.

### Do I have to answer in order?

No. The groups are there so you can pick by mood. Gratitude prompts suit tired days; plan prompts suit Sundays.

### Do gratitude prompts actually work?

Research suggests modest gains in positive mood for some people. Treat them as a small, cheap experiment rather than a cure.

### Can I reuse the same prompt?

Yes, and it is one of the most useful things to do. Comparing two answers months apart shows change better than any single entry.

### What if a prompt brings up something painful?

Stop, close the journal, and do something grounding. If it keeps happening, talk to someone you trust or a professional.

## Bottom line

Journaling prompts for self-improvement work when they are small, honest and repeatable. Save this list, pick one question tonight, and answer it in two lines. The calm tends to arrive before the insight does.
