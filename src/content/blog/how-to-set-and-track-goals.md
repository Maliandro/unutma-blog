Most goals do not fail in a dramatic way. They just stay as a sentence. "Get fit." "Save more." "Learn Spanish." The sentence sits in a note for months because nothing in it tells you what to do on Tuesday at 7pm. Knowing how to set and track goals is mostly about translating that sentence into two things: a few concrete tasks, and one small thing you repeat.

This guide gives you a conversion table for doing that, a 15-minute weekly review to keep it honest, and a short look at what the research supports. The examples use Unutma, which, to be clear, has no dedicated "goals" screen. It has to-dos with subtasks, routines and savings goals, and that turns out to be enough.

## What the research says, briefly

Two findings are worth knowing.

First, **if-then plans help a little**. A 2021 meta-analysis in [Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892/) looked at a method that combines picturing obstacles with plans of the form "if this happens, then I will do that". Across 24 studies it found a small-to-medium effect on reaching goals. Useful, not magic.

Second, **repetition in the same situation builds habits**. A review in the [British Journal of General Practice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3505409/) describes a study in which daily actions took on average around 66 days to feel automatic, with wide variation between people. Missing one day did not derail the process.

Put together: a goal needs a specific plan, and the plan needs a repeated action in a fixed situation.

## The conversion table: goal, tasks, routine

Every goal gets broken into three layers.

| Goal (the sentence) | One-off tasks (to-dos) | Repeated action (routine) | How you know it is working |
|---|---|---|---|
| Get fit | Find a route; buy shoes; book a check-up | Walk 20 minutes, Mon/Wed/Fri after work | Weeks with 3 walks logged |
| Save for a trip | Check flight dates; open a separate account | Move a fixed amount every payday | Savings goal progress |
| Learn Spanish | Pick one course; set a daily slot | 10 minutes after breakfast, weekdays | Days practised per week |
| Read more | Make a list of 5 books | 10 pages before sleep | Books finished per month |
| Call family more | Write down who and how often | Sunday call at 6pm | Calls made this month |
| Sort out paperwork | List what is missing; book an appointment | 15 minutes every Saturday | Items left on the list |

The middle column is where goals usually go to die. If you cannot write a repeated action with a day and a time, the goal is still too vague. Make it smaller until you can.

## Setting goals in Unutma

Here is how each layer maps to the app:

1. **The goal itself.** Add it as a to-do in Lists › To-Do, and set Priority to High so it stays at the top.
2. **One-off tasks.** Open the goal and use "Add subtask" for each step. Give the first one a due date.
3. **The repeated action.** Open Reminders and create a New Routine. Pick the days under "Which Days?" and a time, and choose a reminder type: Gentle, Normal or Alarm.
4. **Money goals.** In the Budget (Economy) tab, "Savings Goals" lets you set a target amount and add contributions.
5. **Wishes that are not goals yet.** Park them in the Wishlist. Not every idea needs a plan this month.

You can also type a routine into the assistant: "walk every Monday, Wednesday and Friday at 6pm" becomes a routine with those days. On the free plan you can have up to 3 routines or reminders, which is honestly a sensible limit for goals anyway.

## The 15-minute weekly review

Once a week, same day, same time:

- [ ] Open your goal to-dos. Is each one still something you want?
- [ ] Check the routine activity for each goal. How many days did it happen?
- [ ] For any routine that happened less than half the time: make it smaller, or move it to a better slot.
- [ ] Tick off finished subtasks and add the next one.
- [ ] Write one if-then plan for the week: "If I skip Wednesday's walk, then I walk on Thursday."
- [ ] Drop or pause one goal if you have more than three active.

Unutma's "Weekly summary" screen shows actions, tasks and journal days for the week, which makes the second step quicker. Our [weekly planning tips](/blog/weekly-planning-tips/) cover the rest of the review.

## Common mistakes

1. **Too many goals at once.** Three is plenty. Five is a wish list.
2. **No repeated action.** Tasks finish; habits carry the goal.
3. **Tracking the outcome only.** You cannot control your weight this week. You can control whether you walked.
4. **Never deleting goals.** Dropping a goal you no longer care about is a decision, not a failure.

## When this approach does not fit

Big, uncertain projects, like changing careers, need more exploration than a routine. Start with tasks that are experiments ("talk to two people in that field") and add a routine once you know the direction. And if a goal is tied to a health condition, set it with your doctor, not with an app.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many goals should I have at once?

Two or three active ones. Park the rest in a wishlist until one finishes.

### What is the difference between a goal and a habit?

A goal is an outcome; a habit is the repeated action that makes it likely. Every goal in the table above has one of each.

### How do I track progress without obsessing?

Track the action, not the result, and check it once a week. Daily checking turns the review into a mood swing.

### How long does it take for a new routine to stick?

In one well-known study, around 66 days on average, with a wide range. Expect about two months, not 21 days.

### What if I miss a week?

Make the routine smaller and start again. The research above suggests occasional misses do not undo progress.

## Bottom line

How to set and track goals comes down to one translation: every goal needs a few tasks and one repeated action with a day and a time. Review it weekly, keep three goals at most, and let the routine do the heavy lifting.
