You check your account at the end of the month and the number is lower than it should be. Nothing big happened. That is exactly the problem: the money went in small, forgettable pieces. Learning how to track personal expenses is mostly about catching those pieces for long enough to see a pattern.

Thirty days is long enough to include a payday, a weekend or four, and at least one "I deserve this" evening. It is short enough to finish. Below is a day-by-day template, three ways of logging that suit different people, and what to look for when the month is done.

## Why 30 days, and why every purchase

The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lists tracking spending as the second of four steps in [building a budget](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/budgeting-how-to-create-a-budget-and-stick-with-it/), right after listing your income, and it stresses that small, frequent purchases count. A week of tracking shows your habits. A month also shows the bills, the irregular costs and the timing.

The rule for the experiment is simple: log everything, change nothing. The first month is for seeing, not fixing. If you start cutting on day 3, you will never know what a normal month looks like.

## Pick a logging method you will actually use

| Method | Good for | Weak point |
|---|---|---|
| Log at the moment of purchase | Accuracy, small cash spends | Easy to forget when busy |
| Log once each evening from receipts and your banking app | People who batch tasks | Cash and small card spends slip through |
| Log by voice right after paying | People who hate typing | Needs a quiet moment |

You can mix them. Most people end up logging big things in the evening and small things at the moment.

## The 30-day template

| Days | Focus | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Set up | Choose six categories: housing, groceries, transport, bills, eating out, other |
| 1 to 7 | Capture | Log every purchase. No judging, no cutting |
| 7 | First look | Which category surprised you? Write one sentence about it |
| 8 to 14 | Capture plus bills | Keep logging. Add every bill you pay this month with its due day |
| 14 | Halfway check | Are you missing anything? Cash, transfers to family, annual fees? |
| 15 to 21 | Capture plus notes | Add a one-word note to purchases you were unsure about ("bored", "tired", "gift") |
| 21 | Pattern check | Look at the notes. Do any words repeat? |
| 22 to 30 | Capture | Keep going. Resist the urge to start budgeting |
| 30 | Review | Total each category and compare with your income |

The one-word notes on days 15 to 21 are the most revealing part. Totals tell you where money went. The notes tell you why.

## What to look for on day 30

- **The biggest category.** Usually housing. Fine. That is expected.
- **The biggest surprise.** Usually eating out, delivery or small online orders.
- **Repeated notes.** "Tired" next to delivery, five times, is a pattern worth knowing.
- **Timing problems.** Bills that land before payday.
- **Forgotten subscriptions.** Anything you would not sign up for again today.

From there, build a first budget. ## Why people quit, and how not to

1. **They miss a day and give up.** Catch up from your banking app the next evening. A gap of a day changes nothing.
2. **They start judging on day 2.** Separate the tracking month from the cutting month.
3. **Logging takes too long.** Cut categories to six and stop writing notes outside days 15 to 21.

If missing days is the main problem, the ideas in [how to build habits that stick](/blog/how-to-build-habits-that-stick/) apply here too: attach the logging to something you already do, such as plugging in your phone at night. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the easiest way to track personal expenses?

Log each purchase within a few minutes of paying, in one place, with six broad categories. Simplicity beats precision in the first month.

### Should I track cash spending too?

Yes. Cash is where most tracking gaps come from. Log it at the moment, because there is no statement to check later.

### Do I need an app?

No. A notebook works. An app helps with totals and reminders, and a manual one keeps your data off third-party servers.

### How detailed should categories be?

Broad. Six categories in the first month. Split one later only if you need to.

### What do I do after the 30 days?

Build a first budget from the totals, set limits for the two categories that surprised you, and track again next month.

## Bottom line

If you want to know how to track personal expenses, the method matters less than the rule: log everything for 30 days and change nothing until it is done. The pattern you find on day 30 is worth more than any budget you could have guessed on day 1.
